A better brief. A better localization.

Prepare a useful localization brief before translating a product video.

01

Start with a specific audience

“Translate this video” leaves several decisions unresolved. Name the target language, the market, the viewing context, and the action you want a viewer to take. A product introduction for a first-time buyer needs a different explanation from a quick reminder for someone who already knows the product.

Write one sentence describing the audience and one sentence describing the purpose. For example: “Introduce a reusable bottle to Vietnamese-speaking shoppers who are comparing everyday accessories. Invite them to look at the available designs.” This is an illustrative brief, not a tested marketing claim.

02

Separate fixed facts from flexible wording

List the information that must survive unchanged: the brand name, model, quantity, material, included accessories, and any stated limitations. Treat product claims carefully. If the source does not promise a result, a localized version should not add one.

Example: a 500 ml bottle

Keep “500 ml” and the product name fixed. The phrase “your everyday companion” can be adapted for natural wording. Do not add insulation, health benefits, or environmental claims that are absent from the source.

Create a small glossary with a preferred translation, an explanation, and words to avoid. Add a pronunciation note for names that will be spoken. A short, agreed glossary is easier to review than discovering conflicting terms after generating the voiceover.

03

Give the translator the missing context

A transcript rarely contains the entire message. A speaker may say “this one” while pointing at a feature. Supply a short description of what is visible, who is speaking, and whether a line is a joke, a product explanation, or a call to action. Keep observations separate from guesses.

For sales slang, write down the intended meaning before choosing a local expression. If a phrase means “simple to carry,” choose language that conveys that idea. Avoid translating an idiom word for word when it would confuse the audience.

04

Include the constraints of the video

Each line has a place on the timeline. Record the start and end time, and note visual events the wording must match. A caption that describes opening a lid should appear with that action. A spoken version may need shorter wording to fit the available segment.

Preserve meaning while editing for length. Do not resolve a timing problem by silently dropping an important limitation or changing a number. Flag the segment for review when a faithful version needs more space.

05

Make the handoff reviewable

Keep the source line, proposed translation, timing, and reviewer notes together. Identify which lines need a language reviewer or a product specialist. A clear handoff makes disagreement easier to resolve because everyone can see the same facts and constraints.

  • Audience, market, and target language are specified.
  • Product facts and glossary terms are recorded.
  • The desired tone and call to action are explained.
  • Timing and relevant visual context accompany each segment.
  • A reviewer knows what to approve and what to question.

In the NexusEngine prototype, subtitles remain editable before voice generation and export. Our planned Claude layer will focus on contextual transcreation; it is not yet implemented. Human review remains part of the intended workflow.
